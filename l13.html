The Great War

Restoring Balance

Lesson Thirteen

Restoring Balance

Technique unlocked The Measure instrumentation, honesty, and restraint

You survived. Now find out what actually happened.

You have four realms of caching, five defenses, and an application that stayed up. You cannot see any of it.

This lesson is about seeing — and about the harder discipline of removing caching that has not earned its place.

Measure hit ratio per key, never globally

A single global hit ratio is close to useless, because it averages away exactly what you need to see.

Global hit ratio: 85%    ← looks healthy

Broken down:
    asset bundles      99.8%     cheap on a miss anyway
    product images     99.1%     cheap on a miss
    product details    94%       moderate
    reviews            78%       moderate
    price               6%       ← expensive, and effectively uncached

That 85% is real, and it is hiding the fact that your most expensive query has essentially no cache at all. The cheap, high-volume things inflated the average until the problem disappeared inside it.

Measure hit ratio per key prefix. A global number can only tell you that everything is fine — which is the one thing you did not need to know.

Also, from Lesson 2: the last few percent are worth the most. Going 90% → 99% removes 90% of your remaining misses. Going 50% → 59% removes 18%. Effort spent on an already-good ratio pays more than effort spent on a bad one — provided the traffic is expensive.

Latency percentiles

A percentile tells you what a given fraction of requests experienced.

  • p50 (the median) — half of requests were faster than this
  • p95 — 95% were faster; the slowest 1 in 20 were worse
  • p99 — 99% were faster; the slowest 1 in 100 were worse

Averages hide caching problems, because caches make most requests fast and a few requests slow — which is precisely the shape an average erases.

Average:  12ms      ← "we're fine"
p50:       2ms      ← the hits
p95:      15ms
p99:     450ms      ← the misses, and every user who hit one

Your p50 is your hit path. Your p99 is your miss path. If p50 is excellent and p99 is terrible, your cache is working and your misses are expensive — that is exactly the case for single-flight and refresh-ahead from Lesson 8.

At scale, p99 is not rare. At 1,000 requests per second, it is 10 users every second, and a user loading a page with 20 requests will very likely hit it at least once.

Memory and eviction rate

From Lesson 10 — the combination is diagnostic:

Hit ratioEviction rateDiagnosis
LowHighCache is too small. Items evicted before reuse.
LowLowTTLs too short, or the data genuinely isn't reused.
HighHighWorking, but under pressure. More memory would help.
HighLowHealthy.

A low hit ratio means very different things depending on the eviction rate, and you cannot tell them apart without both numbers.

Raising a hit ratio deliberately

Four techniques, with honest costs:

1. Adjust key granularity. Caching a whole rendered page gives one entry that must be discarded when anything on it changes. Caching pieces gives more entries, each surviving independently. Finer keys usually mean a higher hit ratio and more lookups.

2. Tune TTLs. Longer TTL, higher hit ratio, more staleness. Pure trade, and the answer comes from the business, not from you. Ask: how wrong can this be?

3. Warm the cache (L2). Attacks the cold-start portion of your misses. Effective after deploys and restarts; does nothing for steady-state misses.

4. Separate hot from cold. If 5% of products get 95% of traffic, cache the hot 5% aggressively — locally, long TTL, refresh-ahead — and barely cache the rest. This usually beats treating everything uniformly, and it is how the hot-key defense in Lesson 12 is built.

And the option people forget: accept the ratio. 60% on data that must be fresh may be the correct answer. Not every number needs improving.

Debugging across four realms

A user reports a stale price. You have four caches. Which one served it?

Without instrumentation this is guesswork. With it, it is a header.

HeaderMeaning
Age: 240This copy has been in a cache for 240 seconds
X-Cache: HIT / MISSCommon CDN convention (non-standard)
Cache-Status: cdn; hit, origin; missThe standard version — reports each cache in the chain

Age is the most useful for stale-data reports. If a user sees a wrong price and Age: 3400, you know it came from a cache with a long TTL and can identify which. If Age: 2, the problem is not staleness — it is your database or your invalidation.

For your own layers, add your own:

X-Cache-Local:  HIT
X-Cache-Redis:  MISS
X-DB-Queries:   3

Not for production users — for you, behind a debug flag, so that “which realm served this?” is a question with an answer.

Also log cache errors separately from misses. They look identical to your application (both mean “go to the database”), and they mean completely different things. A rising cache error rate is a failing cache node. A rising miss rate is a tuning problem. Conflating them hides outages.

Test what happens when the cache dies

The question from Lesson 6, and you should now answer it experimentally.

Turn the cache off in a staging environment under realistic load.

Two possible outcomes:

It gets slower but survives. Good. Your cache is a genuine optimisation.

It falls over. Then what you have is not a cache — it is an undeclared load-bearing dependency with no durability guarantees, and a cache node failure is a full outage.

If the second happens, your options are: size the database for cache-free operation (expensive), add graceful degradation (serve stale, shed non-essential load, queue writes), make the cache highly available, or accept the risk knowingly. All four are legitimate. Not knowing which one you have chosen is not.

What not to cache

The most valuable discipline in the course. Caching is not free — it costs memory, complexity, and correctness. Some things should not be cached at all.

Anything personal, in a shared cache. The cart badge. Lesson 12 attack 5.

Anything genuinely secret. Credentials, tokens, payment details. Not in a shared cache, not on disk, no-store.

Cheap queries. An indexed primary-key lookup served from the buffer pool takes 0.3ms. A Redis lookup takes 0.5ms. You made it slower and added a staleness bug. This is Lesson 7's warning, and it is extremely common.

Data that is written far more than read. Every write invalidates; almost no read benefits. Write-around (L9) and move on.

Data that must be exact. Account balances, remaining stock at the moment of purchase, anything with a legal or financial consequence for being wrong. Read the truth.

Data with no reuse. Caching depends on the same key being asked for again. A per-request unique key is never reused, and caching it is pure cost.

The honest question

For every cache in your system:

What does this layer buy me, and is it worth what it costs?

Cost is memory, code, an invalidation path, a new failure mode, a new way to be wrong, and a permanent increase in how hard your system is to reason about.

Sometimes the answer is a clear yes. Sometimes it is a layer added years ago in front of a query that got an index six months later, and nobody has checked since.

Deleting a cache that no longer earns its place is as much a use of this technique as adding one.

At the Storefront

Instrument all four realms. Then answer, for the last request:

  1. Which realm served it?
  2. How old was the copy?
  3. If it missed, where did the time go?

Then turn off Redis under load and watch what happens.

The misconception to kill

“A higher hit ratio is always better.”

A 99% hit ratio on data that must be fresh is not a success — it is a bug with excellent metrics. The right target comes from what the data is allowed to be wrong about, not from the number being large.
