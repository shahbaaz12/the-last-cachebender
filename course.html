The complete course

The Last Cachebender

A course on caching — what it is, where copies live, and what it costs to keep them. Built around one product page and the seven things on it that each want a different answer.

All fourteen lessons

The Storefront

Every lesson uses the same example application: a product page on an online store.

It has seven parts, and each one wants a different answer. That is why it was chosen.

#Part of the pageHow often it changesPer user?
1Asset bundle — the CSS, JavaScript and logo filesOnly when you deploy new codeNo
2Product imagesRarelyNo
3Product details — name, descriptionRarelyNo
4PriceSometimes, and must be rightNo
5Stock count — “3 left”ConstantlyNo
6ReviewsSlowly, but read a lotNo
7Cart badge — “2 items in your cart”Every time the user actsYes

Watch #7. It is the one part of this page that must never be shared between users. It will come up in every lesson, and by the end you will be able to explain exactly why it was left alone.

Prologue

The Forgotten Art

The world has slowed. Pages take seconds to load. Servers fall over on ordinary afternoons, and people are leaving the realm for faster lands. The Latency Lord rules, and almost nobody remembers that there was ever a way to fight him.

You are about to learn that there is.

There is also a deadline. Word has arrived of the Surge — a wave of traffic the realm cannot avoid and, as things stand, cannot survive.

Lesson One

The Forgotten Art of Caching

Technique unlocked The Sight seeing where time is being wasted

Start with the problem

Open the storefront's product page. It takes 900 milliseconds to appear.

Now open it again. It takes 900 milliseconds again.

Nothing changed between those two visits. The product has the same name, the same description, the same picture. Your server did the exact same work, twice, and got the exact same answer, twice.

That is the waste. Caching is the art of noticing it.

What a cache is

Latency is the time between asking for something and receiving it. When a page is slow, latency is what you are feeling.

A cache (say it “cash”) is a copy of an answer, kept somewhere faster to reach than the place the answer came from.

That is the whole idea. Everything else in this course is detail about where you keep the copy, how long you keep it, and what happens when the copy stops being true.

Why things are slow: two reasons

Almost all slowness comes from one of two causes.

1. Distance. The data is far away and has to travel.

2. Recomputation. The work was already done once, and you are doing it again.

A cache fixes both, in the same way: keep the answer nearby, and stop redoing the work.

How far is “far”?

These numbers are approximate, and they vary by hardware. What matters is not the exact figures but the gaps between them.

Where the data isRoughly how long it takes
In your program's own memory100 nanoseconds
On a local SSD100 microseconds (1,000× slower)
On another server in the same datacentre500 microseconds (5,000× slower)
On a server on another continent150 milliseconds (1,500,000× slower)

Those numbers are too small to feel. So here is the same table again, with every figure multiplied by the same amount.

These are not real durations. Only the ratios are real. If reading from memory took 1 second, then:

Where the data isIf memory took 1 second
Program memory1 second
Local SSD17 minutes
Same datacentre1.4 hours
Another continent17 days

This is the single most useful intuition in caching. Moving an answer from “another continent” to “program memory” is not a small improvement. It is the difference between waiting 17 days and waiting 1 second.

The trade-off

Caching gives you speed, but it charges you in freshness and memory.

You wantWhat caching doesThe cost
SpeedReuses an answer instead of producing it again—
FreshnessMay return an older copyThe answer may be out of date
Low memory useStores additional copiesEvery copy takes space

A cached copy that no longer matches the latest answer is called stale.

Caching buys speed by accepting some risk of stale data and using additional memory. Every technique in this course makes that trade-off differently.

Why caching is a design decision

Caching is sometimes described as an optimisation added after a system becomes slow. That is only partly true.

Adding a cache creates another copy of your data. That copy may fall behind the original.

For a product description, that may be harmless. For a price or stock count, it may cause a customer to see incorrect information.

You do not need to build every cache from the beginning. But you do need to decide which answers are allowed to be old and for how long. That is a correctness decision, so it belongs in the system design.

Look at each part and ask two questions.

What does it cost to produce? How long does it take, and how many database queries does it need? That tells you what is worth caching. This is The Sight — you cannot decide what to cache until you know what is expensive.

How old is it allowed to be? That tells you what you are permitted to cache, which is a different question entirely.

What is allowed to be old?

You have just decided that caching is a promise about what may be out of date. Here is that promise made concrete, part by part.

#PartMay it be stale?If it is, what happens
1Asset bundleYes, indefinitelyNothing — see below
2Product imagesYes, for daysAn old photo shows for a while
3Product detailsYes, for hoursA corrected description appears late
4PriceOnly for secondsA customer sees a price you no longer offer
5Stock countBarely, if at allYou sell something you do not have
6ReviewsYes, for minutesA new review takes a few minutes to appear
7Cart badgeNot applicableA different problem — see below

Notice the shape: the parts that change slowly are safe to keep for a long time, and the parts that change constantly are not. That is the basis of every caching decision you will make in this course.

Two different ways to be wrong

The cart badge is missing from that list, and the reason matters.

Stale means the right answer, from a while ago. £25 when the price is now £20 is stale — it was true once, and it is simply old.

The cart badge fails differently. If a cache hands you my cart badge, that value is not old. It is perfectly current. It just belongs to somebody else.

  • Stale — the right answer, out of date
  • Someone else's — the wrong answer entirely

The first is a trade you can choose to make. The second is never acceptable, at any lifetime, in any shared cache. That is why part 7 is marked never in every table in this course.

One copy, many pages

Look at part 1 again — the CSS, the JavaScript, the logo. It is drawn below the box because it is not really part of this page.

It is part of every page.

The same bundle is used by the home page, the category listing, the search results, the checkout, and the account screen. Cache it once and you have not made one page faster — you have made the whole site faster, for every visitor, on every page after their first.

The same goes for anything that repeats: the header, the navigation menu, the footer, the category list that fills the menu.

The value of a cached copy is not what it saves once — it is what it saves multiplied by how often it is reused.

A product description is reused by everyone who views that product. A logo is reused by everyone who views anything.

And the counter-example is on the same page. The cart badge appears on every page too, exactly like the header does. But “on every page” and “the same for everyone” are different things — and only the second one makes something safe to share.

Lesson Two

The Trial of Hits and Misses

Technique unlocked The Naming keys, values, lifetimes, and warming

This is the heaviest lesson in the Prologue, and that is deliberate. When the Latency Lord attacks in Lesson 12, three of your five defenses come straight out of this lesson. Take your time here.

Hits and misses

You ask the cache for something. Exactly one of two things happens.

  • Hit — the cache has it. You get the answer immediately. No database, no network trip to origin, no recomputation.
  • Miss — the cache does not have it. You must go and get the answer the slow way, and then (usually) put a copy in the cache for next time.

That is the entire vocabulary. Hit: found it. Miss: didn't.

Hit ratio

The hit ratio is the fraction of requests that were hits.

hit ratio = hits ÷ (hits + misses)

If 1,000 requests produce 900 hits and 100 misses, the hit ratio is 900 ÷ 1000 = 0.9, or 90%.

Why it matters — work out the average response time. Say a hit takes 1ms and a miss takes 100ms:

average = (0.90 × 1ms) + (0.10 × 100ms)
        = 0.9ms + 10ms
        = 10.9ms

Two things worth noticing in that number.

First: you went from 100ms to 10.9ms. Nearly ten times faster.

Second, and more important: almost all of the remaining time — 10ms of the 10.9ms — is the misses. At a 90% hit ratio, the 10% that miss are still doing 92% of the waiting.

Now try 99%:

average = (0.99 × 1ms) + (0.01 × 100ms) = 1.99ms

Going from 90% to 99% is another 5× improvement. The last few percent of hit ratio are worth far more than the first fifty. Keep that in mind — it comes back in Lesson 13.

We are only defining hit ratio here. How to deliberately raise it is Lesson 13.

Cold and warm

A cold cache is empty. It has just been created, or restarted, or cleared. Every request misses.

A warm cache has been running long enough to hold the things people actually ask for. Most requests hit.

Every cache starts cold. The period in between — where your app is running but the cache has not filled up yet — is genuinely dangerous, because your database is taking the full load with no protection. This is the cold start problem, and Lesson 12 will show you what an attacker can do with it.

Keys: the most important idea in this lesson

A cache stores pairs. Each pair is:

  • a key — the question
  • a value — the answer

To get something out, you ask with the same key you put it in with. If the key does not match exactly, it is a miss.

So the whole behaviour of your cache depends on how you write your keys. Here is the rule:

The key must contain every input that changes the answer.

Read it again. Nearly every cache bug in this course is a violation of that one sentence.

A key that is too coarse

You cache the product price:

key:   product:42:price
value: 25.00

Looks fine. Then someone visits from Japan and sees ¥25.00 instead of ¥3,800. Or a wholesale customer with a negotiated discount sees the retail price. Or a logged-in member sees the non-member price.

The price depends on currency and customer type. Those inputs changed the answer, and they were not in the key. So the cache handed out an answer to a question nobody asked.

The fix:

key: product:42:price:currency=JPY:tier=wholesale

A key that is too coarse gives wrong answers. This is the worse failure of the two, because nothing crashes — it just quietly lies.

A key that is too fine

Overcorrecting, you add everything you can think of:

key: product:42:price:currency=JPY:tier=wholesale:request_id=a91f3c:time=1699384726

Every request has a different request ID and a different timestamp. So every key is unique. So every request is a miss. You have built a cache with a hit ratio of zero, and it is now slower than having no cache at all, because you also pay the cost of storing everything.

A key that is too fine gives no hits.

The judgement

Good key design sits between those two failures. Include everything that changes the answer. Include nothing else.

When you are unsure, ask: “If two requests have different values for this thing, would they get different answers?” If yes, it goes in the key. If no, it stays out.

Values: what you actually store

The value is what you get back on a hit. Some choices:

How much to store. You can cache the whole rendered product page, or the product record, or just the price. Bigger values mean fewer, larger cache entries; smaller values mean more entries and more flexibility. A cached whole page is fast but must be thrown away when anything on it changes. A cached price survives a description edit.

Whole objects or references. You can store the full review objects, or just a list of review IDs and look them up separately. Storing IDs keeps entries small and avoids duplicating the same review in twenty places — at the cost of a second lookup.

Serialization. Most caches store bytes, not objects. Serialization is turning an object in your program's memory into a sequence of bytes that can be stored or sent, and deserialization is turning it back. This is not free. For small, very hot values, the time spent serializing can be a real fraction of the time you saved — which is one reason keeping a cache inside your own process (Lesson 5) is so fast: no serialization at all.

TTL: how long a copy is allowed to live

TTL stands for time to live. It is the number of seconds a cached copy is considered usable. When the TTL runs out, the copy has expired, and the next request for it is treated as a miss.

key:   product:42:price:currency=USD
value: 25.00
TTL:   300     ← usable for 5 minutes, then thrown away

TTL is your main tool for controlling staleness. A 300-second TTL is you saying: “I accept that this price may be up to five minutes out of date.”

That is a business decision as much as a technical one. Five minutes of a stale product description is fine. Five minutes of a stale stock count might mean selling something you do not have.

TTL is introduced here because you need it from Lesson 3 onward. The policies for what to throw away when memory runs out are Lesson 10 — a related but genuinely different topic.

Warming: filling the cache on purpose

Cache warming is loading data into a cache before anyone asks for it, so the first real user gets a hit instead of a miss.

Three common approaches:

Warm on deploy. After releasing new code, run a script that requests your most important pages so their entries exist before traffic arrives.

Warm from a log. Take the top few thousand keys from yesterday's traffic and load those. Simple and effective, because yesterday's popular items are usually today's.

Lazy warming. Do nothing special — let the cache fill as real requests arrive. This is the default. It is fine for small caches and dangerous for large ones, because the whole warm-up period runs with your database unprotected.

Warming matters most when your cache is large and your database is only just able to cope without it. If your database can handle full traffic with a cold cache, warming is a nicety. If it cannot, warming is the difference between a deploy and an outage.

The Forge

In a room far from the light, the Latency Lord watches a single request pass by. It asks for product 999999999 — a product that does not exist and never has.

He watches it slip past the browser, which has no copy of it. Past the edge, which has no copy of it. Past the application cache, which has no copy of it. And he watches it strike the database directly, and the database dutifully search its entire index for something that was never there.

He watches this happen once. Then he begins writing down names of things that were never real.

The Browser Realm

Your native element. You have bent it already, without knowing the name for it.

Every time you told someone “try a hard refresh,” every time you saw a change you had just made refuse to appear on screen — that was the browser cache, working exactly as designed, and you fighting it blind.

Lesson Three

The Browser Realm

Technique unlocked Windkeeping holding a copy closest to the user

The realm

The browser cache is a store of files kept on the user's own computer by their web browser.

It is the closest cache to the user — there is nothing closer. A hit here involves no network at all. The file is already on their disk. Response time is effectively zero.

It also has a property no other realm has: the storage is free to you. It is the user's disk, not your server's memory. You are not paying for it.

And it has a matching weakness, which will matter enormously in Lesson 11: you cannot reach it. Once you have sent a file to a user's browser with permission to keep it for a year, you have no way to take it back. You cannot delete it. You cannot update it. You can only wait for it to expire.

Everything about browser caching follows from those two facts: it is free and instant, and it is irreversible.

What happens when a page is requested

A page is not one thing the server hands over. It is one file that refers to other files.

The first request. The browser turns the domain into an address, opens a connection, and asks for exactly one thing:

GET /product/42   →   server returns HTML

That HTML contains no images and no styling. It contains references to them:

<link rel="stylesheet" href="/app.a91f3c.css">
<script src="/app.7b2e10.js"></script>
<img src="/headphones.jpg">

The browser then asks for each one separately. Those files can reference further files, so requests arrive in waves:

Time →

[1]  GET /product/42            HTML
      │
      ├─[2]  GET /app.a91f3c.css     stylesheet  ┐
      ├─[3]  GET /app.7b2e10.js      script      │  in parallel
      ├─[4]  GET /logo.svg           image       │
      └─[5]  GET /headphones.jpg     image       ┘
              │
              ├─[6]  GET /inter.woff2      font  ←  the CSS asked for this
              │
              ├─[7]  GET /api/price/42     JSON  ┐
              ├─[8]  GET /api/stock/42     JSON  │  the JavaScript
              └─[9]  GET /api/cart         JSON  ┘  asked for these

A real product page is 50 to 100 requests, not nine.

One connection, many requests

ProtocolConnectionsRequests
HTTP/1.1~6 per domainOne at a time on each — the rest queue
HTTP/2OneAll share it simultaneously
HTTP/3One (over UDP)Same, and one lost packet no longer stalls the others

On a modern site you pay for the connection once — domain lookup and handshakes, roughly 100–300ms — and then dozens of requests flow through it.

Two kinds of response

This is the part that decides where caching rules live.

Who produces itWhere its caching rule is set
Dynamic — /product/42, /api/price/42Your application code runsIn your code
Static — /app.js, /logo.svgA file is read from disk and sentIn configuration

Most of those 50–100 requests are the second kind. The request for /app.js never reaches your application code — it is answered by nginx, or your platform, or a CDN. You configure the rule once, by file type or folder, and it applies from then on.

Which leads to the most important sentence in this lesson:

The browser cache stores files, not pages.

There is no cached copy of your product page. There are sixty independently cached files, each with its own address and its own rule. The storefront's seven parts were never a conceptual division — they are separate HTTP responses, which is exactly why they can have seven different answers.

How caching instructions travel

An HTTP header is a line of information sent alongside a response, separate from the content itself.

HTTP/1.1 200 OK
Content-Type: application/javascript
Cache-Control: public, max-age=86400

(function(){ ...the JavaScript... })

How you control it

You control the browser cache by sending instructions along with your responses. The main one is Cache-Control:

Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000, immutable

That is three separate instructions in one line. Below is each of them, along with the other headers that matter — and where you will actually meet each one.

Reference — expand as needed
max-age=Noptional read

Keep this file and reuse it for N seconds without asking me again.

Cache-Control: max-age=300

For the next 300 seconds, the browser uses its stored copy directly. It will not contact your server at all. Your server does not know those requests happened.

In the wild Universal. Every asset on every major site. Nothing has replaced it.

no-cache and no-store — the classic confusionoptional read

These sound like they mean the same thing. They do not, and mixing them up is one of the most common caching mistakes in the field.

InstructionWhat it actually means
no-storeDo not keep a copy at all. Never write it to disk.
no-cacheKeep a copy, but ask me before using it each time.

no-cache does not mean “do not cache.” It means “cache, but always check first.” It is a caching instruction, not a refusal to cache.

Use no-store for anything genuinely sensitive — a bank statement, a page containing someone's personal details. Use no-cache when you want the speed benefit of a stored copy but need to guarantee it is current.

In the wild Both current. no-store is standard on banking, health and account pages. no-cache is the standard setting for HTML in the fingerprinting pattern further down this lesson.

public and privateoptional read

private means only the user's own browser may store this. Shared caches — the CDN in Book Two, any company proxy in between — must not keep a copy.

public means anyone along the path may store it.

This distinction is a safety mechanism. Anything personalised must be private. The storefront's cart badge is personalised, and if it were ever marked public, a shared cache could store one user's cart and hand it to the next person who came along. That exact failure has a name — cache poisoning — and it is one of the five attacks in Lesson 12.

In the wild Current, and private is load-bearing for security. It is the instruction that stops a shared cache storing an authenticated response.

immutableoptional read

This file will never change. Do not even ask.

Without it, browsers often revalidate stored files when the user hits reload. immutable tells the browser not to bother. It is only safe when the statement is literally true, which brings us to fingerprinted URLs, below.

In the wild Standard on fingerprinted bundles. Firefox and Safari honour it. Chrome never implemented it, having argued that its own reload handling already avoids the problem — so it is harmless where it is ignored.

Expires — the one you should not writelegacy

The predecessor to max-age. Instead of a duration, it carries an absolute date:

Expires: Wed, 21 Oct 2026 07:28:00 GMT

It has an obvious flaw: it depends on the browser's clock agreeing with your server's. max-age counts seconds from when the response arrived, so no clock comparison is needed.

In the wild Legacy. Superseded by max-age, which wins whenever both are present. Some servers still send it alongside max-age for very old clients. You will meet it in real codebases and configuration files. Do not write new ones.

ETag — asking “has this changed?”optional read

max-age covers the period where the browser can skip the server entirely. When that time runs out, the browser would otherwise re-download the whole file — even if it is byte-for-byte identical to the copy it already holds.

An ETag (entity tag) avoids that waste. It is a short identifier your server attaches to a response, acting as a fingerprint of the content. If the content changes, the ETag changes.

Server → Browser:   ETag: "a91f3c"

The browser stores the file along with that tag. Later, when it wants to check:

Browser → Server:   If-None-Match: "a91f3c"      ("I have version a91f3c")

The server compares. If the content still has that fingerprint:

Server → Browser:   304 Not Modified              (no body at all)

304 Not Modified is a response that says “your copy is still good” and contains no content. It is a few hundred bytes instead of a few hundred kilobytes.

If the content has changed, the server sends 200 OK with the new content and a new ETag, as normal.

In the wild Current and everywhere, especially on APIs. One caveat worth knowing: when several servers sit behind a load balancer, ETags generated from file metadata can differ between them, so the same file produces a different tag depending on which server answered — and the browser re-downloads for no reason. ETags derived from the content itself avoid this.

Last-Modifiedoptional read

The same idea as an ETag, using a date instead of a fingerprint. The server sends Last-Modified: <date>; the browser later asks with If-Modified-Since: <date>; the server replies 304 or sends the new version.

In the wild Still sent by most static file servers, usually beside an ETag. It is the weaker of the two — one-second resolution means a file changed twice within the same second looks unchanged. Treat it as a fallback, not a first choice.

stale-while-revalidateoptional read 
Cache-Control: max-age=60, stale-while-revalidate=3600

This says: use the stored copy freely for 60 seconds. After that, for up to an hour, you may still use the old copy immediately — but fetch a fresh one in the background at the same time.

The effect is that the user never waits. They get an instant response from the slightly-stale copy, and the next visitor gets the fresh one.

You are trading a small amount of freshness for the complete removal of waiting. For content where being 60 seconds out of date is harmless, this is close to free performance.

Notice what this is doing: refreshing a value before anyone is blocked waiting for it. That pattern has a formal name — refresh-ahead — and you will meet it properly in Lesson 8. This is its browser-shaped version.

In the wild Current, and growing. It is the mechanism behind Next.js incremental regeneration and the edge caching offered by Vercel and Cloudflare.

Fingerprinted URLs: the trick that makes it all safe

The problem

You ship app.js with Cache-Control: public, max-age=31536000 — one year. That is the right setting: the file is used by every page on the site, and re-downloading it on every visit is pure waste.

Then you find a bug in it. You fix it, you deploy, you refresh the page.

Nothing changes.

The browser has a copy it was told to keep for a year, so it does not ask whether there is a newer one. That is precisely what max-age means. Every user who visited before your fix is holding the broken version, and you have no way to reach them.

The same happens with an image. You upload a corrected product photo over /headphones.jpg. Everyone who already loaded the page keeps seeing the old one.

The solution: never change a file, change its name

Instead of app.js, publish app.a91f3c.js, where a91f3c is derived from the file's contents. Change one character of the code and that value changes, so the filename changes.

app.a91f3c.js     ← still cached, now referenced by nothing
app.5d20e8.js     ← never seen before, must be fetched

The browser is not being persuaded to re-check anything. It is being asked for a file it has never heard of, and it has no choice but to download it.

How does the browser learn the new name?

This is the part that makes the whole scheme work, and it is easy to miss.

The HTML is not cached.

GET /product/42
Cache-Control: no-cache          ← always revalidated
<script src="/app.5d20e8.js"></script>    ← the new name lives here

The HTML is the index. It is small, it is checked every time, and it holds the current names of everything else. The assets are cached forever and never change. The HTML changes constantly and is never cached.

So a deploy spreads like this:

  1. New code produces new filenames
  2. The new HTML references those names
  3. The browser revalidates the HTML — it is no-cache — and receives the new references
  4. Those filenames are unknown to it, so it fetches them
  5. The old files sit unreferenced in the cache until they expire

The user gets the fix on their next page load. No hard refresh, no purge, and you never had to reach into their browser.

Book Two · Water

The Edge Kingdom

Water does not stay where it is put. It flows outward until it surrounds everything.

In Book One you kept a copy in one person's hands. Here you learn to push copies outward across the whole world, until every user in every land has one nearby — and to accept that you no longer hold them.

Lesson Four

The Edge Kingdom

Technique unlocked Tidecall pushing copies outward to the edge

The realm

A CDN — Content Delivery Network — is a large group of servers spread across the world, which keep copies of your content and serve it to whoever is nearest.

Each of those locations is called a PoP (point of presence) or, more commonly, an edge server — “edge” because it is at the outer edge of the network, close to users, rather than at the centre where your own servers are.

Your own server is called the origin — the place the content originally comes from, and the single source of truth behind all the copies.

Why geography is latency

From Lesson 1: a request across a continent takes around 150ms, and that is a physical limit. Light does not go faster because you optimised your code. Physics does not accept pull requests. 🙂

If your origin is in Virginia and your user is in Singapore, every single request pays that crossing. You cannot make the distance shorter — but you can stop making the trip. Put a copy in Singapore and the user's request travels a few miles instead of eight thousand.

And distance is only half of it. A request crossing the planet passes through dozens of routers, any of which can be congested — rather like a long-haul flight, where the hours in the air are the part you can predict, and the connections, the customs queue and the delay on the tarmac are the part you cannot.

That is all a CDN is. It does not make anything faster. It makes things closer.

How a request flows

User in Singapore
      ↓
Nearest edge server (Singapore)
      ├─ HIT  → serve the stored copy                        ~5ms
      └─ MISS → fetch from origin (Virginia)                 ~200ms
                store the copy at the Singapore edge
                serve it
                ↓
                next Singapore user gets a HIT               ~5ms

The first Singapore visitor pays full price. Everyone after them gets the local copy.

The edge is loading missing data for you, on its own, without your application being involved. That behaviour has a name — read-through caching — and you will meet it formally in Lesson 8. A CDN is a read-through cache you did not have to write.

Cache keys at the edge

Lesson 2's rule applies here with full force: the key must contain every input that changes the answer. But at the edge, you are not the one building the key — the CDN is. Your job is to configure what goes into it.

By default the key is roughly the URL. You can add parts to it or take parts away — and both of Lesson 2's mistakes are waiting for you here.

Put too much in the key and identical pages get stored separately, so nothing is ever reused.

Put too little in and different answers collide under one key, so people get somebody else's page.

The edge has a characteristic version of each.

Query parameters: too much in the key

A query parameter is the part of a URL after ?, like ?page=2 or ?utm_source=newsletter.

Some of them genuinely change the response. /search?q=headphones and /search?q=laptop are different pages, and the CDN must treat them as different keys — otherwise everyone searching for anything gets the first person's results.

So most CDNs include the whole query string in the key by default. That is the safe choice. It is also the one that quietly destroys your hit ratio.

Because if the CDN includes all of them, then these are four different keys:

/product/42
/product/42?utm_source=newsletter
/product/42?utm_source=twitter
/product/42?fbclid=IwAR2x9...

Four identical pages, stored four times, each starting cold. Marketing links and social-media click identifiers generate near-infinite unique URLs, so your hit ratio collapses toward zero for exactly the traffic you spent money to attract.

Key normalisation is the fix: tell the CDN which query parameters actually affect the response, and to ignore everything else. page matters. utm_source does not.

The Vary header: when the URL is not enough

Query parameters are at least visible in the URL. But some responses differ based on things that are not in the URL at all: the language the browser asked for, whether it accepts compressed content, whether the user is signed in.

The URL is identical. The right answer is not. The cache has no way to know that by itself.

Vary is how you tell it. The header declares that a response depends on something in the request, not just the address.

Vary: Accept-Language

This says: the answer differs depending on the language the browser asked for — so store a separate copy per language. Without it, the first visitor's language would be served to everyone. That is the too-little-in-the-key failure, and it is a real bug that ships regularly.

But Vary is dangerous in the other direction too:

Vary: Cookie

This says store a separate copy for every distinct cookie value. Since almost every visitor has a unique session cookie, almost every visitor gets their own private copy that is never reused. You have turned a global cache into a very expensive way of caching nothing.

Vary: Cookie is a well-known way to accidentally disable a CDN entirely.

Changing what the edge holds

You have pushed copies out across the world. Now the price changes. How do you fix thousands of copies you do not control?

Three options, and the differences matter.

MethodHow it worksSpeedCost

TTL is the default and covers most cases. Pick a TTL you can live with being wrong for.

Purge is the emergency tool — a wrong price, a legal takedown. It is not instant and not unlimited; CDNs restrict how often you can purge, so a design that purges on every write will hit a wall.

Versioning is the strongest option, and the same trick as Book One's fingerprinted filenames. Never edit; publish a new name. Nothing to invalidate, because nothing was ever changed.

That last idea is important enough that it returns as a general technique in Lesson 11, under the name versioned keys.

Origin shield

Here is a problem that only appears once you are big.

You have 200 edge locations. A popular item's TTL expires. All 200 edges now have a miss at roughly the same moment, and all 200 go to your origin for the same file.

Your origin, which was comfortably serving a trickle of traffic, gets 200 simultaneous requests for one thing.

An origin shield is one designated edge server that sits between all the others and your origin. Edges do not go to origin — they go to the shield. The shield gets one miss, makes one origin request, and answers the other 199 from its copy.

Your origin sees one request instead of 200.

Hold on to this shape: many simultaneous misses on one key, all hitting the thing behind the cache. It is the exact structure of the cache stampede, the first attack in Lesson 12, and the origin shield is one form of the defense.

Reverse-proxy caching

A reverse proxy is a server that sits in front of your application and answers requests on its behalf. nginx and Varnish are the common ones.

It is the same idea as a CDN, with one difference: it runs on your own hardware, usually in your own datacentre, rather than being distributed worldwide.

  • A CDN reduces latency by being geographically close to users.
  • A reverse proxy reduces load by absorbing repeat requests before they reach your application.

Same mechanics — cache keys, TTLs, purging, Vary. Different reason for existing. Many systems run both: a CDN worldwide, and a reverse proxy in front of the origin behind it.

The misconception to kill

“The CDN caches my site.”

It does not cache your site. It caches individual responses — the same point as Lesson 3, one layer further out — and only the ones your headers permit, under keys you configured.

Nobody chooses to put a session cookie in the cache key, or to let a tracking parameter split one popular page into fifty thousand cold ones. It happens because a default was never examined. The CDN did exactly what it was told. The instruction was simply never read.

The Forge

The Latency Lord does not look at the content the edge is holding. He looks at how the edge decides that two requests are the same request.

He notices a header being included in the key that should not be. He notices another that should be included and is not.

He does not need to break into the Edge Kingdom. He only needs to be mistaken for someone else.

Book Three · Earth

The Application Sanctum

Air was free but beyond your reach. Water flowed outward and left your hands.

Earth is different. Earth is ground you stand on and control completely — your own memory, your own rules, your own decisions about every byte.

First you learn to hold. Then you learn to split stone: one cache spread across many nodes, all of them agreeing.

Lesson Five

The Application Sanctum

Technique unlocked Stonehold holding data in your own memory

Scope of this lesson: one process, on one machine. Caching across many servers is Lesson 6. Caches the database keeps for itself are Lesson 7. Keeping these separate matters, because they fail in completely different ways.

The realm

An in-memory cache — also called a local cache or in-process cache — is data your application keeps in its own memory, inside the running program.

A process is one running copy of your program. When you start your server, that is a process. It has its own memory that no other program can see.

This is the fastest cache that exists. There is no network. There is no serialization — the object in the cache is a real object in your program, ready to use. A hit takes roughly 100 nanoseconds.

What you can hold here

You now have somewhere very fast to put things. The question is what to put there.

Inside your own process, two different kinds of thing are worth keeping — and they carry completely different risks.

Work you already did. A calculation, a parsed file, a rendered fragment. The inputs are still sitting right there in your program; you simply do not want to do the arithmetic twice. This can only go stale if the inputs change, and you control the inputs.

Data that came from somewhere else. A product row from the database, a response from another service. This copy can become wrong at any moment, because somebody else can change the original without telling you.

The first is nearly free of risk. The second is where Lesson 11 comes from.

Start with the safe one.

Memoization

Memoization is caching the result of a function so that calling it again with the same inputs returns the stored answer instead of recomputing it.

calculate_shipping(weight=2.5, zone="EU", express=false)
   → first call:  runs the calculation      12ms
   → second call: returns the stored answer  0.0001ms

The inputs form the key. The return value is the value. It is Lesson 2's key rule applied to a function signature — and it has the same failure mode. If the function secretly depends on something not in its arguments (today's date, a config setting, a database row), then that hidden input is missing from your key, and you will serve the wrong answer.

Memoization is only safe for functions where the same inputs always produce the same output.

The costs

Memory is shared with your application. A local cache lives in the same memory your app needs to run. An unbounded cache will consume it all and crash the process. Always set a maximum size — how the cache decides what to remove when it hits that limit is Lesson 10.

The cache dies with the process. Restart, deploy, crash, or scale down, and it is gone. Every deploy gives you a cold cache and the full cold-start problem from Lesson 2.

It does not scale with your data. A local cache can hold what fits in one machine's spare memory. That is a hard ceiling.

Redis, introduced carefully

Redis is a separate program that stores key-value data in memory and is reachable over a network. Many applications can talk to the same Redis instance.

It is important to be precise about this, because the terms get muddled constantly:

Local cacheRedis
Where it livesInside your processA separate program, usually another machine
Access time~100 nanoseconds~500 microseconds
SerializationNone — real objectsYes, every read and write
Survives restartNoYes
Shared between serversNoYes

Redis is roughly 5,000 times slower than a local cache. It is also roughly 200 times faster than a cross-continent request, and — the reason it exists — it is shared.

Both are “caching in the application layer.” They are not the same realm at all, and treating them as interchangeable is a source of real confusion.

What belongs in a local cache

Good candidates are small, hot, read-mostly, and tolerant of being slightly stale:

  • Configuration and feature flags
  • The category tree
  • Currency conversion rates
  • Results of expensive pure calculations
  • Very hot lookups you would otherwise send to Redis thousands of times a second

Bad candidates are large data, anything that must be exactly current, and anything personal to one user — because your process serves many users, and a key that forgets to identify the user will hand one person's data to another.

At the Storefront

Memoize the shipping and price calculations. Keep the category tree in a local cache with a 5-minute TTL.

Leave the product data alone for now. You are about to find out why.

The misconception to kill

“In-memory cache means Redis.”

Local is inside your process — no network, no serialization, gone on restart, invisible to your other servers. Redis is across a network and shared. The gap between them is three orders of magnitude, and the shared part is the entire reason the next lesson exists.

The closing hook

You now run two application servers behind a load balancer, because one was not enough.

An administrator changes a product's price from £25 to £20.

Server A handles the write and updates its local cache. Server B knows nothing about it and still holds £25.

Refresh the page a few times. £20. £25. £20. £25. The price changes depending on which server happens to answer.

Two caches. One key. Two different values. Which one is telling the truth?

Neither. That is the problem, and it is what Stonesplit exists to solve.

Lesson Six

The Distributed Cache Tribes

Technique unlocked Stonesplit one cache spread across many nodes

The problem, stated properly

You have 10 application servers. Each has its own local cache. This gives you three distinct problems.

1. They disagree

The scenario that ended Lesson 5, now with ten servers instead of two. It is worth walking through slowly, because “the same code produced two different answers” sounds impossible.

The code is identical on every server. What differs is when each one last looked.

09:00   Server A   reads price £25 from the database, caches it
09:00   Server B   reads price £25 from the database, caches it

09:30   Admin      changes the price to £20 in the database
        Server A   handled that write, so it updates its own cache   → £20
        Server B   was not involved. It has no idea anything happened → £25

09:31   Customer   request lands on Server A    → sees £20
09:31   Customer   refreshes, lands on Server B → sees £25

Neither server has a bug. Both are running exactly the code you wrote. They disagree because a cache is a copy, and each server made its own copy at its own moment.

So with ten servers you do not have one cache with a stale entry. You have ten separate caches, each stale in its own way.

2. Your hit ratio collapses

The same product must be loaded and stored ten separate times — once per server. Each server only sees a tenth of the traffic, so each takes far longer to warm up. You are paying ten times the memory for a fraction of the benefit.

3. You cannot invalidate

Change something, and you would need to reach into all ten processes to remove the stale copy. There is no mechanism to do that. As you add servers, it gets worse.

A distributed cache solves all three: one shared cache that every server reads from and writes to.

Server A ─┐
Server B ─┼─→  Shared cache  ─→  Database
Server C ─┘

One value. One place to update. One warm-up. All ten servers benefit from the first one's miss.

In practice, for most teams, that shared cache is Redis. So the rest of this lesson is about what Redis actually gives you, where it genuinely shines, and what it costs.

What Redis gives you

You met Redis in Lesson 5 as “a separate program that stores key-value data in memory.” Here is what that buys you beyond simply being shared:

  • Every server sees the same value. The reason this lesson exists.
  • It survives your application restarting. Deploys no longer empty your cache.
  • Sub-millisecond reads. Slower than local memory, far faster than a database.
  • TTL is built into every key. You do not implement expiry — you set it and Redis honours it.
  • Eviction is built in. When memory fills, Redis decides what to drop using a policy you choose (Lesson 10).
  • Operations are atomic. Two servers incrementing the same counter cannot overwrite each other, because the increment happens inside Redis rather than in your code.
  • It can tell your servers when something changed. Redis can publish a message that every server receives — the mechanism behind event-driven invalidation (Lesson 11).
  • It stores more than strings. Counters, lists, sets and sorted sets are native, which makes some problems trivial that are awkward anywhere else.

Where Redis is genuinely excellent

ScenarioWhy Redis specifically
Session storageEvery server needs the same session. The original use case, and still the most common.
Rate limitingAtomic counters with an expiry — correct under concurrency without you writing locks
Counters and leaderboardsSorted sets do natively what is genuinely awkward in a relational database
Shared cache in front of a databaseThe case this course is about
Simple queuesLists as job handoff between services

A few of these in practice:

Session — store it with a 30-minute expiry
    SET session:a91f3c "{...}" EX 1800

Rate limiting — count this minute's requests, expire the counter after it
    INCR   ratelimit:user:42:0930
    EXPIRE ratelimit:user:42:0930 60

Leaderboard — record a score, then read the top ten instantly
    ZADD      leaderboard 4820 "player:17"
    ZREVRANGE leaderboard 0 9

Caching — store a product for five minutes
    SETEX product:42 300 "{...}"
    GET   product:42

Look at the rate-limiting example. Two servers can run INCR on the same key at the same moment and the count is still correct, because Redis performs the increment itself. Doing that with a plain read-modify-write in your own code — read 10, add 1, write 11, twice, concurrently — loses one of the increments.

This is the read-modify-write race, and Redis solves it by doing the work for you.

How Redis scales

Three mechanisms, and you mainly need to know that they exist and what they cost.

Replicas. Copies of the data on other nodes. They serve reads and they mean a dead node does not take its data with it. The cost is replica lag — the gap between a write landing on the primary and appearing on the replica. Usually milliseconds, never zero, and during that window a read from a replica returns the old value.

Redis Cluster. When one machine's memory is not enough, Cluster splits your keys across several nodes. Every key is assigned to one of 16,384 hash slots, and each node owns a range of slots. Add a node and it takes over some slots from the others.

The sentence to take away:

Your key lives on exactly one node.

Failover. When a primary dies, one of its replicas is promoted. Two things this costs you: any writes that had not yet reached the replica are lost, and there is a gap of seconds while the failure is detected and the promotion happens, during which requests to that node fail.

Which leads to a rule you should design around now:

Your application must keep working when the cache is unavailable. Slower, but working. Treat a cache error as a miss, not as an exception.

If it cannot do that, what you have built is not a cache. It is a database with no durability guarantees. Lesson 13 will make you test this.

The downsides

Redis is not free, and the costs are worth stating plainly:

  • It is a network hop. Roughly 5,000 times slower than local memory. Do not send it a request you could have answered in-process.
  • It is another thing that can go down. See the rule above.
  • Memory is expensive. RAM costs far more per gigabyte than disk. A cache that holds everything is usually a cache that costs more than the problem.
  • Everything is serialized. Objects go in as bytes and come back as bytes, and that conversion is not free.
  • It is not a database. Redis can persist to disk, but that is not what it is for. Do not store anything there that you cannot afford to lose.
  • One key lives on one node. A single very popular key concentrates all of its traffic on one machine, and adding nodes does not help — the key still lives in exactly one place.

That last one is not a flaw you can configure away. It is a property of how sharding works, and the Latency Lord is about to notice it.

At the Storefront

Move product details, price, and reviews to shared Redis. Pick a key scheme and a shard key. Keep the category tree local — it is small, it is read constantly, and slight staleness is harmless. Two realms, used together, on purpose.

The misconception to kill

“A shared cache makes my data consistent.”

It makes your servers agree with each other. All ten can now confidently serve the same stale price. Agreement is not correctness, and the gap between them is Lesson 11.

The Forge

The Latency Lord counts the nodes and studies the traffic flowing to each.

They are evenly spread — except for one. A single key, asked for more often than every other key combined. The front-page item. The one everybody wants.

He does not need to attack every node in the cluster. He needs the one node that holds that key.

The Database Depths

Fire is the most powerful realm, and the one you build in least.

It is already burning. You did not light it and you cannot put it out. Your work here is not to build, but to see what is already alight — and to stop adding fuel where none is needed.

Lesson Seven

The Database Depths

Technique unlocked Emberdraw drawing on the database's own heat

The realm

You have been treating the database as “the slow place behind the cache.”

That is not really what it is. Your database is already a sophisticated caching system, built by people who spent decades on this exact problem.

The buffer pool is memory the database sets aside to hold recently used data. Databases do not read individual rows from disk — they read pages, fixed-size blocks containing many rows, and those pages stay in memory afterwards. On a well-configured database the buffer pool holds the entire working set — the part of your data actually being used — and its hit ratio is above 99%. Underneath it, the operating system keeps its own cache of recently read disk blocks.

The query plan cache stores the strategy for running a query — which index to use, what order to join tables — so the same query shape is not re-planned every time. Note carefully: it caches the plan, not the results. The query still runs.

Put those together and most of what you think of as “reading from the database” never touches a disk at all.

You tune here, you do not build

This realm is unlike the other three. In the browser you set headers. At the edge you configure keys. In your application you write the caching code yourself. Here, the caching already exists and is largely automatic.

What you actually control:

LeverWhat it does
IndexingThe big one. A precomputed structure for finding rows without scanning the table
PartitioningSplit one large table into smaller pieces so a query touches less of it
ShardingSplit the data across machines when one is no longer enough
Buffer pool sizingGive the database enough memory to hold the working set

Most of the answer, most of the time, is the first three. A query that is slow because it scans a million rows does not need a cache in front of it. It needs an index, or a partitioning scheme that means it only ever looks at this month's data.

Why an index beats a cache

An index is a data structure the database maintains so it can find rows without examining every row in the table. Without one, finding all reviews for product 42 means checking every review in the table — a full table scan. With one, the database goes straight to them.

That makes an index a form of precomputation, which sounds cache-adjacent. But it has two properties no cache has:

  • It is never stale. The database updates it as part of the write. It cannot disagree with the data.
  • There is nothing to invalidate. No TTL, no purge, no event to publish, no race to lose.

So there is a question worth asking before every cache you add:

Is this query slow because it is genuinely expensive, or because it is missing an index?

If the answer is a missing index, adding the index is not merely an alternative — it is strictly better. It is faster, it is always correct, and it introduces no staleness. Caching a query that needed an index means taking on every difficulty in Lesson 11 to avoid a problem you could have deleted.

The same applies to the N+1 problem — loading fifty products with one query and then their reviews with fifty more: caching those fifty lookups hides the waste, while loading the reviews alongside the products in a single query removes it.

An index is not free — it makes writes slightly slower and it uses disk. But it is paid for in a currency you understand, once, rather than in correctness, forever.

Before you cache in front of a database

Two things to do first.

Measure. You may find the product query takes 0.8ms, served entirely from the buffer pool, while your Redis lookup takes 0.5ms. That cache bought you 0.3ms and a staleness problem. This is The Sight from Lesson 1, applied where it is easiest to skip.

Then read the following, twice.

A cache that was removed because it could not be kept correct

MySQL used to have a query cache: same query text, same result handed back without executing anything. It was deprecated, and then removed entirely.

The reason is the most useful thing in this lesson.

Any write to a table had to invalidate every cached result that touched that table. On a busy system, tracking which results those were cost more than simply running the queries again. Under concurrent load the cache itself became the bottleneck, with every request queuing on the lock that protected it.

Now read that again with Lesson 11 in mind.

This was a cache built by database engineers, living inside the database, with perfect knowledge of every write at the instant it happened. They concluded that keeping it correct was not worth what it cost.

They had every advantage you are never going to have. In Lesson 11 you will build an invalidation scheme from outside the database, with no such knowledge, inferring when things change from the code paths you remembered to instrument.

That is not an argument against caching. It is an argument for knowing exactly what you are taking on — and for reaching first for the index, which has no invalidation problem at all.

At the Storefront

Before caching the product query anywhere, check two things: whether it is already being served from the buffer pool, and whether it uses an index.

You may discover the cache you were about to add is a cache in front of a cache.

The misconception to kill

“The database is slow, so I need a cache in front of it.”

The database is already caching aggressively, at several levels, and it is very good at it. Measure before adding a layer — otherwise you are paying in staleness for a problem you did not have.

Lesson Eight

The Three Reading Techniques

Technique unlocked The Three Draws cache-aside, read-through, refresh-ahead

The question

Every read has the same shape: the data might be in the cache, or it might not. What happens on a miss?

There are three answers, and the difference between the first two is who is responsible.

Cache-aside

Cache-aside — also called lazy loading — means your application manages the cache directly. It is the most common pattern in use, and the default you should assume unless there is a reason otherwise.

value = cache.get(key)

if value is not None:
    return value                 # HIT

value = database.query(key)      # MISS — application fetches
cache.set(key, value, ttl=300)   # application stores
return value

The application talks to both the cache and the database, and it owns every step.

Why people use it:

  • Only requested data is cached. Nothing is stored that nobody wants.
  • The cache going down is survivable — cache.get fails, you read the database, the app is slower but alive.
  • It works with any cache and any database. No special library required.
  • You can see exactly what is happening.

The costs:

  • Every miss pays three trips: check cache, query database, write cache.
  • The logic is repeated everywhere you cache something, and someone will eventually get it wrong.
  • It has a specific race condition, which is the second half of this lesson.

Read-through

Read-through means your application only ever talks to the cache. The cache knows how to load missing data by itself.

value = cache.get(key)    # cache handles the miss internally
return value

You configure the cache with a loader function — the code that fetches from the database — and the cache calls it when it needs to.

The distinction is exactly this:

Who handles the miss
Cache-asideYour application
Read-throughThe cache layer

That is the whole difference. Not performance, not freshness — responsibility. And because it decides where the logic lives, it decides where the bugs live.

Why people use it: the loading logic exists once, not scattered everywhere. Application code becomes simple. Many caching libraries do the hard parts (like the race below) for you.

The costs: you need a library that supports it. The cache going down is now harder to survive, because your app has no other path to the data. And the loading logic is one step further from the code that uses it.

You have already met one. In Lesson 4, the CDN fetched from origin on a miss without your application knowing. A CDN is a read-through cache. You did not implement it, so it did not feel like a pattern. It was.

Refresh-ahead

Both patterns above are reactive: a user hits a miss, and that user waits while the data loads.

Refresh-ahead is proactive. For values you know are hot, refresh them before they expire, so no user ever encounters the miss.

TTL = 300 seconds, refresh at 240

t=0    value cached
t=240  ─→ background job refreshes it   (no user is waiting)
t=245  value replaced, timer resets
       ...no user ever sees a miss on this key

Why people use it: for hot keys, misses effectively disappear. Your p99 latency — the slow tail you will meet in Lesson 13 — improves dramatically, because the tail was the misses.

The costs: you refresh values that may never be requested again, so you do work for nothing. You need to identify which keys deserve it. Applying it to everything is a way to generate constant pointless load.

You have met this one too. stale-while-revalidate from Lesson 3 is refresh-ahead expressed as an HTTP header: serve the old copy now, refresh behind it, nobody waits.

Which realm uses which

RealmNatural patternWhy
BrowserCache-first with revalidationControlled by headers, not code
CDNRead-throughFetches from origin on a miss automatically
Local cacheCache-aside or memoizationYou wrote it, you own it
RedisCache-asideThe usual default
Database buffer poolRead-throughThe database handles its own misses

Notice the pattern: when you own the code, you get cache-aside. When someone else built the cache, you get read-through. The techniques are the same everywhere; only the ownership moves.

The race: many readers, one missing key

Here is the flaw in cache-aside, and it is not a rare edge case — it is a guaranteed event on any popular key.

The front-page product is requested 1,000 times per second. Its cached value expires.

t=0.000   TTL expires
t=0.001   Request 1    → miss → queries database
t=0.001   Request 2    → miss → queries database
t=0.001   Request 3    → miss → queries database
   ...
t=0.001   Request 1000 → miss → queries database
t=0.050   first query returns, cache is written
          — 49 milliseconds too late for the other 999
One thousand identical database queries, for one value.

The cache did not fail. Every one of those requests followed cache-aside correctly. The pattern has a gap between “the value expired” and “the value has been reloaded,” and every request arriving in that gap goes to the database.

Worse, this is self-amplifying. A thousand simultaneous queries slow the database, which makes the reload take longer, which widens the gap, which lets more requests through.

Single-flight

Request coalescing — usually called single-flight — fixes it: when many requests need the same missing key, one of them fetches it, and the rest wait for that one result.

t=0.001   Request 1    → miss → acquires the loader slot → queries database
t=0.001   Request 2    → miss → someone is loading → waits
t=0.001   Request 3    → miss → someone is loading → waits
   ...
t=0.050   Request 1 gets the value, stores it, and hands it to all 999 waiters

One database query. All 1,000 requests served.

The shape:

def get(key):
    value = cache.get(key)
    if value is not None:
        return value

    # only one caller per key gets past this point;
    # the others block here and receive the same result
    with single_flight(key):
        value = cache.get(key)        # check again — someone may have
        if value is not None:         # filled it while we waited
            return value
        value = database.query(key)
        cache.set(key, value, ttl=300)
        return value

Two details that matter:

  • The second cache.get is not redundant. By the time a waiter is released, the value usually exists. Checking again avoids a pointless second query. This is the double-checked pattern.
  • The lock is per key, not global. A global lock would make every miss on every key wait behind every other, which would be far worse than the problem.

Most mature caching libraries provide this. It is worth knowing it exists before you need it — and you are about to need it.

Remember this. This is your first weapon against the Latency Lord, and it is deliberately being handed to you four lessons before the fight.

At the Storefront

Cache-aside for product details, with single-flight — it is the front page item and it will stampede.

Refresh-ahead for the category tree — small, hot, expensive, on every page.

Nothing for the cart badge, in any pattern.

The misconception to kill

“Cache-aside and read-through are the same thing.”

They differ in who owns the miss, and that decides who owns the race condition above. With cache-aside, it is yours to solve. With read-through, your library probably solved it — which is a genuine reason to prefer one.

The Forge

The Latency Lord has been watching the reading techniques, and he has found the gap.

Not a flaw in the cache. A gap in time — the space between the moment a value expires and the moment it is restored. It is small. Fifty milliseconds, perhaps.

A thousand requests can arrive in fifty milliseconds.

He does not need to take the cache away. He only needs to choose the moment it empties.

Lesson Nine

The Three Writing Techniques

Technique unlocked The Three Seals write-through, write-behind, write-around

The problem

Reading was straightforward: the value is there or it is not.

Writing is harder, because now two places hold the same fact — the cache and the database — and a write must deal with both. Between the moment you update one and the moment you update the other, they disagree.

There are three strategies. They differ in when the database is written and whether the cache is written at all.

Write-through

Write-through writes to the cache and the database together, synchronously, before reporting success.

Application → write to cache
            → write to database
            → both confirmed → return success

Why people use it: the cache is never stale relative to the database, because they are updated together. A subsequent read is guaranteed correct. Reasoning about the system is simple.

The costs:

  • Writes are slower. You pay for both systems on every write.
  • You cache things nobody reads. Every write populates the cache, whether or not that item will ever be requested. On write-heavy data with few reads, you fill your cache with dead weight and evict things people actually want.
  • It is not actually atomic. Writing to two systems without a transaction spanning both means one can succeed and the other fail. If the cache write succeeds and the database write fails, your cache now holds a value that does not exist in the source of truth — which is worse than being stale.

Where you would use it

  • Account and profile settings. Someone changes their display name and lands straight back on a page showing it. The cache is already correct when that page renders.
  • Feature flags and configuration. Written rarely, read on nearly every request, and every server needs the new value at once.
  • Stock counts on a low-volume catalogue. Being wrong means overselling, and the write rate is low enough that the extra latency does not matter.
  • Shopping cart contents. Written, then immediately read on the next page, every time.

The pattern: read-after-write is common, and being wrong is worse than being slow.

Write-behind

Write-behind — also called write-back — writes to the cache, returns success immediately, and writes to the database later in the background.

Application → write to cache → return success   ← user is done here
                             ↓
                    (later) write to database

Why people use it:

  • Writes are very fast. The user waits for a memory write, not a disk write.
  • Writes can be batched. A counter updated 1,000 times in a second becomes one database write of the final value. For write-heavy workloads this is a genuine order-of-magnitude reduction in database load.
  • Sudden write spikes get absorbed instead of overwhelming the database.

The cost, stated plainly: you can lose data.

If the cache dies between the acknowledgement and the database write, that write is gone. You told the user it succeeded. It did not.

This is not a rare edge case to design around — it is the fundamental trade. Write-behind is correct for data where losing some is acceptable. It is wrong for orders, payments, and inventory.

Where you would use it

  • View counts. A page viewed a million times an hour becomes a handful of database writes instead of a million.
  • “Last seen” and “last active” timestamps. Updated on every single request, and nobody notices if one is a minute stale or lost entirely.
  • Likes, reactions, and vote tallies. High volume, and an occasional lost increment is invisible.
  • Analytics and telemetry events. Losing a fraction of a percent does not change any dashboard anyone looks at.

The pattern: enormous write volume, and nobody files a support ticket if a few writes vanish.

The test to apply: if thirty seconds of these writes disappeared in a crash, would anyone notice? If the answer is yes, this is not your strategy.

Write-behind is not a faster write-through. It is a different guarantee. Write-through promises durability. Write-behind promises speed, and explicitly does not promise durability.

Write-around

Write-around writes only to the database and does not populate the cache at all. Usually it also removes any existing cached copy, so a stale one cannot survive.

Application → write to database → return success
              (cache entry deleted if present)
              next read misses and loads fresh

Why people use it: write-heavy data that is rarely read soon after writing does not pollute the cache. Your cache stays full of things people are actually reading.

The cost: the next read of that item is a miss. If your write pattern is “write then immediately read” — which is extremely common, since users usually look at what they just did — write-around gives you a guaranteed miss on every one.

Where you would use it

  • Bulk imports and migrations. Loading a million rows overnight. Filling the cache with all of them would evict everything real users actually want — the exact scan problem you will meet in Lesson 10.
  • Audit logs and event records. Written constantly, read almost never, and usually only during an investigation.
  • Historical and archival data. An order from three years ago is written once and may never be read again.
  • Draft autosave. A user saves every few seconds; only the final version is ever read by anybody else.
  • Sensor and IoT readings. High write volume, queried later in aggregate rather than individually.

The pattern: written far more often than read, or not read again for a long time.

Choosing

SituationStrategyBecause
Read soon after write, must be correctWrite-throughCache is right immediately
Very high write rate, some loss acceptableWrite-behindSpeed and batching
Written often, read rarelyWrite-aroundKeeps the cache useful
Read-heavy, occasionally writtenWrite-around + cache-asideSimple, safe, and the common default

That last row deserves attention. Write-around paired with cache-aside — write to the database, delete the cached copy, let the next read reload it — is the most widely used combination in practice, because it is simple and it fails safely. It is also, under a different name, the subject of Lesson 11.

Which realms can write

An important limit: the browser and the CDN do not participate in business writes.

When a user changes their address, that write goes to your application and your database. The browser cache and the CDN are caches of responses. They are not in the write path at all — you cannot “write-through” to a CDN.

They deal with writes only by being told their copies are no longer valid, which is expiry, purging, or versioning. Everything in this lesson is really about the application layer — your local cache, Redis, the database.

Read and write strategies describe the relationship between an application, a cache, and a source of truth. Two of the four realms do not have that relationship.

At the Storefront

Stock count — decrements constantly, and being wrong means selling something you do not have. Write-through, so the cache is never ahead of reality. Or do not cache it at all, which is a legitimate answer.

Review submission — written rarely, read constantly. Write-around: store it, drop the cached review list, let the next reader rebuild it.

Product view counter — written on every page view, read almost never, and losing a few is fine. Write-behind, batched.

Three write paths, three different strategies, on one page.

The misconception to kill

“Write-behind is just a faster write-through.”

It trades durability for speed. If the cache dies before the flush, the write is gone — after you told the user it succeeded. Choose it deliberately, for data you can afford to lose.

Lesson Ten

The Temple of Eviction

Technique unlocked The Letting Go expiry and eviction

Two different reasons data leaves

Things disappear from a cache for two reasons, and the two have nothing to do with each other.

  1. Expiration — the copy got old, so you stopped trusting it.
  2. Eviction — you ran out of room, so something had to go.

That is the whole distinction. It is worth stating that plainly, because the two are confused constantly — including in documentation.

TriggerReasonControlled by
ExpirationTimeThe copy is too old to trustTTL
EvictionMemory pressureYou ran out of roomEviction policy

Expiration is time-driven. It is about correctness. The value may still be perfectly useful and there may be plenty of memory — you remove it because you no longer believe it.

Eviction is memory-driven. It is about capacity. The value might be perfectly current and useful — you remove it because something else needs the space.

A cache with infinite memory still needs expiration. A cache holding data that never changes still needs eviction. They are independent, and confusing them leads to setting TTLs to manage memory (which makes your data needlessly stale) or sizing memory to manage freshness (which does not work at all).

TTL, used deliberately

You met TTL in Lesson 2. Now use it for two distinct jobs.

TTL as a correctness tool. The TTL is your statement about how wrong you are willing to be. TTL = 300 says: I accept this may be up to five minutes out of date. Pick it from the business requirement, not from habit.

TTL as a safety net. This one is subtle and important. Even with perfect invalidation (Lesson 11), things go wrong — a message is dropped, a service is down, a code path is missed. TTL bounds the damage. Without it, a failed invalidation means a value is wrong forever. With it, the worst case is bounded by the TTL.

Put a TTL on everything, even data you invalidate explicitly. It is the backstop for the day your invalidation silently fails. It will.

TTL jitter

Set 10,000 keys during a deployment, all with TTL = 3600.

One hour later, all 10,000 expire in the same second.

Jitter is adding a small random amount to each TTL so they spread out:

ttl = 3600 + random(0, 600)     # between 60 and 70 minutes

Now those 10,000 keys expire spread across ten minutes rather than in a single instant. This single line prevents a specific attack in Lesson 12, and it costs you nothing.

Eviction policies

When the cache is full and something new arrives, something old must go. The eviction policy decides what.

Reference — expand as needed
LRU — Least Recently Usedoptional read

Remove whatever has not been touched for the longest time.

The logic: recently used things tend to be used again soon. Usually true, which is why LRU is the sensible default and what you should choose absent a reason.

The weakness — and it is severe. LRU is destroyed by a scan: one large pass over data that will not be requested again. A nightly report reading a million products marks all of them “recently used,” pushing out the small hot set that your actual users depend on. The report finishes; your cache is full of things nobody wants; your hit ratio collapses; your database absorbs the difference.

A single batch job can wipe out a perfectly healthy cache. Watch for this.

Where you would use it Almost everywhere, as the default. It is what Redis allkeys-lru gives you, what most application cache libraries do without being asked, and what your CPU does with its own caches. Session data fits it especially well — a user who was active a minute ago is the one most likely to be back. Reach for something else only if you have batch jobs sweeping the cache, or a hot set stable enough for LFU to protect.

LFU — Least Frequently Usedoptional read

Remove whatever has been requested least often, regardless of when.

The logic: popularity is more meaningful than recency. A page requested 10,000 times matters more than one requested once, even if the once was more recent.

Why it can beat LRU: it survives scans. The report's million products each get one access; the front-page item has thousands. LFU keeps the front page and discards the report data — exactly right.

The weaknesses:

  • New items look unpopular. Something added a second ago has a count of 1 and is a prime eviction target, even if it is about to become the most popular item on the site. New content struggles to establish itself.
  • Old items overstay. Something enormously popular last month keeps a high count long after people stopped wanting it.

Practical implementations use windowed or decaying LFU, counting recent accesses more heavily, which fixes both. If you choose LFU, choose one of these.

Where you would use it When a small, stable set of items takes most of the traffic — the top 1% of products that account for most views, a CDN holding assets that stay popular for months, reference data that barely changes. It is also the right answer when you have a nightly batch job that would otherwise flush an LRU cache. Avoid it where content turns over quickly: news, social feeds, anything where today's popular item was created this morning.

FIFO — First In, First Outoptional read

Remove whatever was added earliest, regardless of use.

Simple and cheap — no bookkeeping. It also ignores usage entirely, so it will happily evict your most popular item because it happened to be cached first. Rarely the right choice for a cache; fine for a queue.

Where you would use it Honestly, rarely, and almost never for a general cache. Where it does fit is anywhere insertion order is the point rather than an accident — a fixed-size buffer of recent events, a replay window, a rolling log kept in memory. If every entry has roughly equal value and you want zero bookkeeping, FIFO is defensible. If some entries are more valuable than others, it is the wrong tool.

Randomoptional read

Remove a randomly chosen item.

This sounds like giving up, and it is not. It requires no bookkeeping at all, which makes it very fast and memory-efficient — LRU must maintain ordering on every single access, and that costs something on every read.

For workloads without a strong recency pattern, random performs surprisingly close to LRU at a fraction of the overhead. Redis offers it deliberately. Do not dismiss it.

Where you would use it Very high-throughput caches where LRU's per-read bookkeeping is a measurable cost, and memory-constrained systems where the ordering structure itself is too expensive to keep. It also does well where access is genuinely uniform and no hot set exists to protect. Redis exposes it as allkeys-random for exactly these cases.

Choosing

PolicyBest whenFails when
LRUAccess is bursty and recency-drivenA scan sweeps through it
LFUThere is a stable hot setContent turns over quickly
FIFOAlmost never for cachesAny time popularity matters
RandomLow overhead matters; access is uniformA clear hot set deserves protecting

Real caches offer combinations — Redis has allkeys-lru, volatile-lru (only keys with a TTL set), allkeys-lfu, volatile-ttl (shortest remaining life first), and noeviction (reject writes when full).

volatile-* variants matter more than they look: they let you mark some entries as never-evictable by giving them no TTL, protecting data you cannot afford to lose.

Reading the eviction rate

The eviction rate — how many items are being removed per second for lack of space — is one of the most useful signals a cache gives you.

  • Near zero: your cache is big enough. Good.
  • High and rising: your cache is too small. Items are being thrown out before they can be reused, and your hit ratio is being destroyed by capacity, not by TTL.
  • Sudden spike: something just flooded the cache. A batch job, a scan, a bug writing far too much.

A high eviction rate paired with a falling hit ratio is a specific, actionable diagnosis: the cache needs more memory, or you are caching too much. Lesson 13 will show you where to watch this.

The misconception to kill

“LRU is always the right choice.”

LRU is a good default that a single batch job can destroy. LFU survives scans but starves new content. Random is cheaper than both and often nearly as good. Choose from your access pattern, not from habit.

Lesson Eleven

The Invalidation Paradox

Technique unlocked The Truthbinding keeping the copy true to the source

The hardest problem in the course

The moment you cache something, the same fact exists in two places:

  • The original, in your database. This is the truth.
  • The copy, in your cache. This is what your users actually see.

Two places holding one fact can disagree — and when they do, the cache has no idea. Nothing tells it the database moved on without it.

Staleness is a cached copy no longer matching the truth it was copied from. It is not an error state — a stale cache does not crash, log a warning, or fail a health check. It quietly serves a wrong answer with complete confidence.

Lesson 7 showed you that database vendors removed their query caches because keeping them correct was too expensive. That is how hard this is. You are now going to solve it anyway, because you must.

Three questions

Before choosing a technique, answer these for the data in question:

1. How wrong can it be? A view count off by three is fine. A price off by £5 is a legal problem.

2. For how long? Seconds, minutes, hours?

3. What does being wrong cost? A stale description is an annoyance. A stale stock count is an oversold order, a refund, and an angry customer.

These answers give you your TTL and tell you how much effort invalidation deserves. Data that can be five minutes stale needs a TTL and nothing else. Data that must be correct within a second needs real machinery.

Invalidate-on-write

The standard approach, and the natural partner to cache-aside from Lesson 8:

On write:
    database.update(key, new_value)
    cache.delete(key)

On the next read:
    cache miss → load from database → cache the fresh value

Update the truth, remove the copy, let it rebuild on demand.

Delete, do not update

Given a write, you could either delete the cached copy or update it in place with the new value. Updating seems better — it avoids the next reader missing.

It is not better, and here is why.

Two administrators change a price at nearly the same time:

t=0   Writer A: database ← 10
t=1   Writer B: database ← 12
      Database is now 12.  ✓ correct

t=2   Writer B: cache ← 12
t=3   Writer A: cache ← 10        ← arrived late
      Cache is now 10.  ✗ WRONG

The database ended at 12, correctly. But the two cache writes arrived in the opposite order — network timing, thread scheduling, a garbage-collection pause, any of a dozen reasons. The cache now holds 10 and will keep serving it until its TTL runs out.

Now the same sequence with deletion:

t=0   Writer A: database ← 10,  cache.delete
t=1   Writer B: database ← 12,  cache.delete
t=2   Reader:   cache miss → reads database → gets 12  ✓ correct

Deletes cannot arrive out of order in a harmful way, because a delete carries no value. Whatever order they land in, the result is the same: no cached copy, and the next read gets the truth from the source.

Delete on write. Do not update on write. It costs one extra miss and removes an entire category of bug that is nearly impossible to reproduce.

Versioned keys

A different approach: never invalidate anything. Change the name instead.

product:42:v7    ← everyone reads this
                   price changes
product:42:v8    ← everyone reads this now

The old entry is not deleted. It simply becomes unreferenced and expires on its own.

Why this is powerful:

  • No invalidation race. Nothing is being deleted, so nothing can be deleted at the wrong moment.
  • No purge cost. No message needs to reach every cache node.
  • It works in realms you cannot reach. You cannot delete anything from a user's browser cache — but you can serve a page referencing v8, and the old entry becomes irrelevant. This is the only mechanism that works for the browser realm.
  • Bulk invalidation becomes free. Put a version in a namespace prefix and increment it, and thousands of keys become unreachable in one operation.
catalog:v3:product:42
catalog:v3:product:99
catalog:v3:category:8
    ...increment to v4 and every catalog key is invalidated at once

The costs: you must track the current version and get it to everyone who builds keys. Old entries occupy memory until they expire, so you are paying for garbage for a while.

You have now seen this three times: fingerprinted filenames in Lesson 3, versioned URLs in Lesson 4, versioned keys here. It is the same technique, and it is the strongest tool in this lesson.

Event-driven invalidation

With many servers and many caches, one server performing a write cannot delete the copies held by the others.

Event-driven invalidation broadcasts the change:

Server A: writes to database
          publishes "product:42 changed"
                     ↓
          ┌──────────┼──────────┐
      Server B   Server C   Server D
      deletes    deletes    deletes
      local      local      local

Sources for these events:

  • Application publishes after a successful write. Simple. Misses anything that changes the database another way — an admin tool, a migration, a manual fix.
  • Database change feed — the database itself reports every change (PostgreSQL logical replication, MySQL binlog, MongoDB change streams). Catches everything, no matter who wrote it. More setup.
  • Database triggers publishing on write. Catches everything, but puts application logic in the database.

The trade-off is honest: application-published events are easy and incomplete; change feeds are complete and more work. If anything other than your application ever writes to your database, you need the change feed.

Invalidating across all four realms

Here is where the whole course converges. A price changes. Where does the old value live?

RealmHow you invalidateHow fastCan you force it?
BrowserNot possible — TTL expiry or a versioned URLUp to the full TTLNo

Local cachesEvent broadcast to every processMillisecondsYes, with machinery

Read the first row again, because it governs your whole design:

You cannot reach the browser cache. Once you have told a browser to keep something for an hour, it is gone for an hour. There is no recall.

This is the reason the storefront's price header in Lesson 3 was max-age=60 and not max-age=3600. Your browser TTL is not a performance setting — it is a hard limit on how fast a correction can possibly reach your users. Set it to the longest wrong-answer window you can defend.

And it is the reason versioned URLs matter so much: they are the only way to make a change reach a browser instantly, because they do not require reaching the browser at all.

The repopulation race

One more trap, and it defeats people who have done everything else correctly.

t=0   Reader:  cache miss → begins reading database → gets 10
t=1   Writer:  database ← 12
t=2   Writer:  cache.delete("price")        ← correctly invalidated
t=3   Reader:  cache.set("price", 10)       ← writes the stale value it
                                              fetched at t=0

The cache now holds 10, was correctly invalidated, and will serve the wrong value until its TTL expires. Every step followed the rules. The reader was simply holding a stale value in flight when the delete happened.

Mitigations:

  • Versioned keys avoid it entirely — the reader writes to v7, everyone is reading v8. This is the cleanest answer.
  • Delayed double delete — delete, then delete again after a short delay, to catch in-flight writers.
  • Short TTLs bound the damage without preventing it.
  • Single-flight with locking (Lesson 8) narrows the window considerably.

TTL as the safety net

Every technique in this lesson can fail. Messages get dropped. Services restart mid-broadcast. Somebody adds a write path and forgets the invalidation. The repopulation race happens.

TTL is what stops any of those becoming permanent.

Put a TTL on everything. Invalidate explicitly for speed. Let TTL be the guarantee that a failure is temporary rather than forever.

That sentence is the practical summary of this lesson.

Attack 1 — The Stampede

Midnight. The front-page product's cached value expires. In the next 50 milliseconds, 1,000 requests arrive. Every one misses. Every one queries the database.

The database, which was comfortable, receives 1,000 identical queries at once. It slows down. Because it slows down, the reload takes longer. Because the reload takes longer, more requests pile into the gap. Because more requests pile in, the database slows further.

Your database falls over. Not because of a flaw in your cache — because your cache worked exactly as designed. Cache-aside has a gap between “expired” and “reloaded,” and every request arriving in that gap goes to the database.

Also called a dogpile or thundering herd.

You saw this being forged · Lesson 8

He does not need to take the cache away. He only needs to choose the moment it empties.

TechniqueFromWhat it does

Single-flight is the direct answer. Refresh-ahead is the preventive one. Use both on your hottest keys.

Attack 2 — The Penetration

Requests arrive for product:999999999. It does not exist and never has.

Follow it through your realms. The browser has no copy — nothing was ever stored. The CDN has no copy. Redis has no copy. So the request reaches the database, which searches its index and confirms the product does not exist.

Then the same request arrives again. Every step repeats. A miss is cached; a “does not exist” is not.

Your entire caching architecture is transparent to these requests. Every one goes straight to the database, and an attacker can send thousands per second using randomly generated IDs. Your cache defends nothing, because there is nothing to cache.

You saw this being forged · Lesson 2

He begins writing down names of things that were never real.

Stop here and work out the defense before reading on.

Negative caching — cache the fact that something does not exist.

value = cache.get("product:999999999")

if value is NOT_FOUND_MARKER:
    return 404                          # HIT — never touches the database

if value is None:                       # genuinely not in cache
    row = database.query(...)
    if row is None:
        cache.set(key, NOT_FOUND_MARKER, ttl=60)   # ← cache the absence
        return 404
    cache.set(key, row, ttl=300)
    return row

Where this comes from: L2 taught that a key maps to a value, and L8 taught cache-aside. The insight is that “there is no such product” is a perfectly valid answer, and valid answers can be cached. Nothing new — just noticing that a negative result is still a result.

Two details:

  • Use a short TTL. If someone creates product 999999999 tomorrow, you do not want to serve a stale 404 for an hour. Sixty seconds is typical.
  • Use a distinct marker. You must be able to tell “cached as non-existent” apart from “not in the cache.” Storing plain null usually makes those indistinguishable, and you get a permanent miss instead of a hit.

Also worth applying: input validation. If product IDs are always under 8 digits, reject 999999999 before it reaches any cache or database at all. The cheapest defense is not answering the question.

Attack 3 — The Avalanche

You deployed at 14:00 and warmed the cache — 50,000 keys, all written within a few seconds, all with TTL = 3600.

At 15:00, all 50,000 expire in the same second.

Every request now misses. Not one hot key — everything, simultaneously. Your database receives your entire read traffic with no cache in front of it, for as long as it takes to reload 50,000 entries. It cannot cope, because it was never sized to; the cache was absorbing 95% of the load.

The same thing happens when a cache node restarts, or when a cluster fails over and comes back empty.

The distinction from a stampede: a stampede is many requests, one key. An avalanche is many keys, all at once.

You saw this being forged · Lesson 10

He simply finds every expiry timer in the kingdom and sets them all to the same hour.

Attack 4 — The Hot Key

You partitioned your cache across 10 nodes. Load is evenly spread — except that one key is not like the others.

The front-page item receives 40% of all cache traffic. So one node receives 40% of your traffic while the other nine share the remaining 60%.

That node saturates. Its latency rises. Every request for the popular item — the item most of your revenue depends on — is now slow. The other nine nodes are nearly idle.

Adding more nodes does not help. The key still lives on exactly one node. Sharding distributes keys; it cannot distribute one key.

You saw this being forged · Lesson 6

He does not need to attack every node in the cluster. He needs the one node that holds that key.

Stop here and work out the defense before reading on.

1. A local shield cache (L5). Keep the hot key in each application server's own memory, in front of the shared cache. Ten app servers each holding a copy means the shared cache receives ten requests instead of a million. A 5-second local TTL is usually plenty. This is the single most effective fix, and it is exactly the point of Lesson 5.

2. Key splitting (L2). Store the same value under several keys and pick one at random per request:

product:42:copy0
product:42:copy1
   ...
product:42:copy9      ← 10 keys, one value, spread across 10 nodes

Reading picks a random copy, so traffic distributes. Writing must update all ten, so this is for read-heavy data only.

3. Replication (L6). Put the hot partition on replicas and spread reads across them.

4. Detection first (L13). You cannot defend a hot key you have not found. Per-key metrics are how you find them — which is next lesson.

Notice that this attack is defeated by using two realms together: a local cache in front of a distributed cache. That is what Book Three was building toward.

Attack 5 — The Poisoning

The worst one, because it is a correctness failure rather than a performance failure — and it can be a security incident.

A logged-in user requests the product page. The response includes their cart badge: “2 items.” Something in your stack caches that response under the key /product/42, without the user in the key.

The next visitor requests /product/42. They get a hit. They see someone else's cart.

That is cache poisoning: incorrect or unauthorised content stored in a cache and served to everyone.

How it happens in practice:

  • A key missing a distinguishing input — the Lesson 2 failure, at scale
  • Vary not set on a response that varies by language, currency, or authentication
  • private not set on a personalised response, so a shared cache stores it
  • An error page cached — a 500 error gets a 1-hour TTL and now everyone sees an error page for an hour
  • Unnormalised request headers — an attacker sends a header your app reflects into the response, and the poisoned version is cached and served to others

He does not need to break into the Edge Kingdom. He only needs to be mistaken for someone else.

If a response differs per user, it must never be stored in a shared cache. Ever.

Look at the five attacks together:

Every one of these happens at ordinary scale on an ordinary weekday. Avalanche in particular is usually self-inflicted, by a deploy, on a Tuesday afternoon, with no attacker involved at all.

Lesson Thirteen

Restoring Balance

Technique unlocked The Measure instrumentation, honesty, and restraint

You survived. Now find out what actually happened.

You have four realms of caching, five defenses, and an application that stayed up. You cannot see any of it.

This lesson is about seeing — and about the harder discipline of removing caching that has not earned its place.

Measure hit ratio per key, never globally

A single global hit ratio is close to useless, because it averages away exactly what you need to see.

Global hit ratio: 85%    ← looks healthy

Broken down:
    asset bundles      99.8%     cheap on a miss anyway
    product images     99.1%     cheap on a miss
    product details    94%       moderate
    reviews            78%       moderate
    price               6%       ← expensive, and effectively uncached

That 85% is real, and it is hiding the fact that your most expensive query has essentially no cache at all. The cheap, high-volume things inflated the average until the problem disappeared inside it.

Measure hit ratio per key prefix. A global number can only tell you that everything is fine — which is the one thing you did not need to know.

Also, from Lesson 2: the last few percent are worth the most. Going 90% → 99% removes 90% of your remaining misses. Going 50% → 59% removes 18%. Effort spent on an already-good ratio pays more than effort spent on a bad one — provided the traffic is expensive.

Latency percentiles

A percentile tells you what a given fraction of requests experienced.

  • p50 (the median) — half of requests were faster than this
  • p95 — 95% were faster; the slowest 1 in 20 were worse
  • p99 — 99% were faster; the slowest 1 in 100 were worse

Averages hide caching problems, because caches make most requests fast and a few requests slow — which is precisely the shape an average erases.

Average:  12ms      ← "we're fine"
p50:       2ms      ← the hits
p95:      15ms
p99:     450ms      ← the misses, and every user who hit one

Your p50 is your hit path. Your p99 is your miss path. If p50 is excellent and p99 is terrible, your cache is working and your misses are expensive — that is exactly the case for single-flight and refresh-ahead from Lesson 8.

At scale, p99 is not rare. At 1,000 requests per second, it is 10 users every second, and a user loading a page with 20 requests will very likely hit it at least once.

Memory and eviction rate

From Lesson 10 — the combination is diagnostic:

Hit ratioEviction rateDiagnosis
LowHighCache is too small. Items evicted before reuse.
LowLowTTLs too short, or the data genuinely isn't reused.
HighHighWorking, but under pressure. More memory would help.
HighLowHealthy.

A low hit ratio means very different things depending on the eviction rate, and you cannot tell them apart without both numbers.

Raising a hit ratio deliberately

Four techniques, with honest costs:

1. Adjust key granularity. Caching a whole rendered page gives one entry that must be discarded when anything on it changes. Caching pieces gives more entries, each surviving independently. Finer keys usually mean a higher hit ratio and more lookups.

2. Tune TTLs. Longer TTL, higher hit ratio, more staleness. Pure trade, and the answer comes from the business, not from you. Ask: how wrong can this be?

3. Warm the cache (L2). Attacks the cold-start portion of your misses. Effective after deploys and restarts; does nothing for steady-state misses.

4. Separate hot from cold. If 5% of products get 95% of traffic, cache the hot 5% aggressively — locally, long TTL, refresh-ahead — and barely cache the rest. This usually beats treating everything uniformly, and it is how the hot-key defense in Lesson 12 is built.

And the option people forget: accept the ratio. 60% on data that must be fresh may be the correct answer. Not every number needs improving.

Debugging across four realms

A user reports a stale price. You have four caches. Which one served it?

Without instrumentation this is guesswork. With it, it is a header.

HeaderMeaning
Age: 240This copy has been in a cache for 240 seconds
X-Cache: HIT / MISSCommon CDN convention (non-standard)
Cache-Status: cdn; hit, origin; missThe standard version — reports each cache in the chain

Age is the most useful for stale-data reports. If a user sees a wrong price and Age: 3400, you know it came from a cache with a long TTL and can identify which. If Age: 2, the problem is not staleness — it is your database or your invalidation.

For your own layers, add your own:

X-Cache-Local:  HIT
X-Cache-Redis:  MISS
X-DB-Queries:   3

Not for production users — for you, behind a debug flag, so that “which realm served this?” is a question with an answer.

Also log cache errors separately from misses. They look identical to your application (both mean “go to the database”), and they mean completely different things. A rising cache error rate is a failing cache node. A rising miss rate is a tuning problem. Conflating them hides outages.

Test what happens when the cache dies

The question from Lesson 6, and you should now answer it experimentally.

Turn the cache off in a staging environment under realistic load.

Two possible outcomes:

It gets slower but survives. Good. Your cache is a genuine optimisation.

It falls over. Then what you have is not a cache — it is an undeclared load-bearing dependency with no durability guarantees, and a cache node failure is a full outage.

If the second happens, your options are: size the database for cache-free operation (expensive), add graceful degradation (serve stale, shed non-essential load, queue writes), make the cache highly available, or accept the risk knowingly. All four are legitimate. Not knowing which one you have chosen is not.

What not to cache

The most valuable discipline in the course. Caching is not free — it costs memory, complexity, and correctness. Some things should not be cached at all.

Anything personal, in a shared cache. The cart badge. Lesson 12 attack 5.

Anything genuinely secret. Credentials, tokens, payment details. Not in a shared cache, not on disk, no-store.

Cheap queries. An indexed primary-key lookup served from the buffer pool takes 0.3ms. A Redis lookup takes 0.5ms. You made it slower and added a staleness bug. This is Lesson 7's warning, and it is extremely common.

Data that is written far more than read. Every write invalidates; almost no read benefits. Write-around (L9) and move on.

Data that must be exact. Account balances, remaining stock at the moment of purchase, anything with a legal or financial consequence for being wrong. Read the truth.

Data with no reuse. Caching depends on the same key being asked for again. A per-request unique key is never reused, and caching it is pure cost.

The honest question

For every cache in your system:

What does this layer buy me, and is it worth what it costs?

Cost is memory, code, an invalidation path, a new failure mode, a new way to be wrong, and a permanent increase in how hard your system is to reason about.

Sometimes the answer is a clear yes. Sometimes it is a layer added years ago in front of a query that got an index six months later, and nobody has checked since.

Deleting a cache that no longer earns its place is as much a use of this technique as adding one.

  2. How old was the copy?
  3. If it missed, where did the time go?

“A higher hit ratio is always better.”

A 99% hit ratio on data that must be fresh is not a success — it is a bug with excellent metrics. The right target comes from what the data is allowed to be wrong about, not from the number being large.

The end

The Surge arrives.

It arrives at the browser cache, and most of it stops there — never reaching the network at all.

What continues arrives at the edge, spread across the world, and is answered locally by copies already waiting.

What continues past that arrives at the application, and finds its answers in memory. The keys that are hot are already held close. The values approaching expiry have already been refreshed. The thousand requests that arrive for one missing key send one query and share its answer.

What reaches the database is a trickle. The database, which has been quietly caching all along, does not even notice.

And the Latency Lord, who built five weapons out of your own timers and your own keys, watches every one of them break against decisions you made eleven lessons ago.

He was never defeated by a new technique.

He was defeated by someone who had measured first.
