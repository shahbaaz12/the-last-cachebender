The Great War

The Final Battle

Lesson Fourteen

The Final Battle

Technique unlocked The Convergence all four realms in one design

The task

Design the complete caching strategy for the storefront, across every realm.

You are not learning anything new here. Every piece of this was built in an earlier lesson. This is assembly, not invention — and if it feels like assembly, the course worked.

The rubric

#RequirementFrom
1Every one of the seven parts assigned to a realm — or marked never cached, with a reasonL1–L7
2A cache key and a TTL for each cached partL2, L10
3A read strategy and a write strategy for the application layerL8, L9
4An invalidation path for every mutable part, across every realm that holds itL11
5A named defense for each of the five attacksL12
6An instrumentation plan — what you measure, what alerts youL13
7A stated failure mode: what happens when the cache diesL6, L13

A worked example

Here is requirement-complete coverage of one element — the price — as a model for the shape expected. Do the other six yourself.

Element: Price

1. Realms — Browser, CDN, Redis. Not local cache: it changes too often to risk per-server divergence.

2. Keys and TTLs

RealmKeyTTLWhy
BrowserURL60s + stale-while-revalidate=30060s is the hard ceiling on how fast a correction reaches users (L11)
CDNURL + currency + customer tier300sPurgeable, so a longer TTL is safe
Redisprice:{id}:{currency}:{tier}300s + jitterJitter prevents avalanche (L10)

Currency and tier appear in every key that has a key at all. They change the answer, so they are in the key (L2).

3. Read and write

  • Read: cache-aside with single-flight — it is a hot key and it will stampede (L8)
  • Write: write-around — update the database, delete the cached copy (L9)
  • Delete, never update — concurrent writes can land out of order (L11)

4. Invalidation path

Admin changes price
    ↓
Database updated                                    immediate
    ↓
Redis DELETE price:42:*                             immediate
    ↓
CDN purge /product/42                               ~seconds
    ↓
Browser: no mechanism — bounded by the 60s TTL      up to 60s

Worst-case staleness: 60 seconds, entirely in the browser realm, entirely because that realm cannot be reached (L11). Everything reachable is corrected in under a second.

5. Attack defenses

AttackDefense
StampedeSingle-flight + refresh-ahead on top-100 products (L8)
PenetrationNegative caching, 60s TTL, plus ID validation (L12)
AvalancheTTL jitter on every price key (L10)
Hot key5-second local cache in front of Redis for top products (L5)
PoisoningCurrency and tier in the key; public is safe only because price is not per-user (L2, L12)

6. Instrumentation

  • Hit ratio for the price: prefix specifically — not the global number (L13)
  • p99 latency on price lookups; alert if it exceeds 100ms
  • Alert on stale-price reports with Age attached, to identify the realm
  • Track invalidation event failures — a silent failure here is a wrong price for 60 seconds

7. Failure mode

Redis unavailable → every price read goes to the database. The local 5-second cache absorbs the hot products. Database load rises roughly 4×, which is within capacity. Degraded but alive. Tested under load in staging.

Do the other six

Product details. Product images. The asset bundle. Stock count. Reviews.

And the cart badge — for which the complete answer is: never cached, in any realm, at any layer, because it differs per user and Lesson 12 attack 5 shows exactly what happens if you get that wrong.

It has been in every table since Lesson 1, always marked “never.” Requirement 1 is satisfied not by caching it, but by being able to say precisely why you did not.

The final assessment

When you can produce that document for a real system — and defend every TTL in it — you have finished this course.

The test is not whether you can list caching techniques. It is whether you can look at a piece of data and say: this belongs here, for this long, invalidated this way, and here is what happens when it goes wrong.

The end

The Surge arrives.

It arrives at the browser cache, and most of it stops there — never reaching the network at all.

What continues arrives at the edge, spread across the world, and is answered locally by copies already waiting.

What continues past that arrives at the application, and finds its answers in memory. The keys that are hot are already held close. The values approaching expiry have already been refreshed. The thousand requests that arrive for one missing key send one query and share its answer.

What reaches the database is a trickle. The database, which has been quietly caching all along, does not even notice.

And the Latency Lord, who built five weapons out of your own timers and your own keys, watches every one of them break against decisions you made eleven lessons ago.

He was never defeated by a new technique.

He was defeated by someone who had measured first.
